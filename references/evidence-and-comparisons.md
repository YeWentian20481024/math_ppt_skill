# Presenting evidence and comparisons

Use this reference when the supplied talk includes experiments, measurements, or comparative results. It guides how to communicate existing evidence; it does not prescribe a research design or require an experiment in every mathematical talk.

## Give the audience enough context

Before displaying results, state the question, the problem being studied, and the conditions needed to interpret the comparison. Show the relevant mathematical definitions rather than only a case nickname. A common setup page can serve several result pages; do not repeat every configuration option on every slide.

Identify what varies, what remains fixed, what is measured, and the comparison reference. If several factors vary together, describe the tested family without attributing the outcome to just one factor. Name compared approaches by what was actually evaluated.

Give each experiment or comparison a distinct role in the argument. Explain which claim it supports and which broader claim would require more evidence. A component-level observation does not automatically establish the behavior of a whole system.

## Define the meaning of the reported quantities

Do not treat different indicators as interchangeable without a supporting argument. State what each measures, how it is defined, and why it matters for the claim on the slide. Distinguish an internal decision criterion from an independent assessment of the result.

Describe the reference and its limitations. Agreement between two approaches establishes consistency, not necessarily correctness. If an approximation or modification changes the problem being answered, make that change visible beside the affected comparison.

Separate observations, established explanations, and proposed interpretations. Do not turn a plausible cause into a demonstrated mechanism through slide wording. Identify missing support without inventing results or launching unrequested experiments.

## Compare like with like

State the conditions under which a cost, quality, or reliability comparison is meaningful. Equal nominal settings do not necessarily imply equal achieved quality or resources. Label resource measures precisely rather than substituting one for another.

Keep unfavorable outcomes, incomplete runs, and exclusions visible when they affect the conclusion. Do not present progress on an unfinished task as a same-task speedup over a completed task. Match comparison conditions, observation points, and normalization where required.

When repeated measurements are available, identify how they are summarized and show uncertainty relevant to the claim. Do not imply statistical significance from a single observation. Label versions when changes affect results; a successful local check does not establish that all broader results remain valid.

## Make the visual answer a question

Define axes, units, legends, and reference markers as applicable. Use a detail view, difference view, or table when the main plot cannot reveal the relevant distinction. Explain magnification and avoid scales that overstate practical importance. Use labels or line styles in addition to color.

For a trend, state what varies, how the response is measured, and which range supports the interpretation. Distinguish observed trends from theoretical predictions. Show uncertainty or gaps where they change the conclusion.

Keep enough context on audience slides for the result to be understood. Place secondary configuration and extended interpretation in notes or backup pages. When pruning, preserve the definitions and conditions needed by the remaining results.
